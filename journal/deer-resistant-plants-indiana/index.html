Deer-Resistant Plants for Indiana Gardens (and What Deer Will Eat Anyway)

No plant is deer-proof, but some are much less appetizing. Here’s what tends to survive in central Indiana, and how to protect the rest.

If you garden anywhere near a woods, a creek or a farm field in Montgomery County, you probably know deer. They browse hostas to the ground, nip the buds off daylilies the night before they open, and strip tender new growth from shrubs. This guide covers plants deer usually leave alone, which favorites need protection, and strategies that actually help.

First, what "deer-resistant" really means

No plant is deer-proof. Deer are browsers with broad tastes, and what they eat varies with the season, how hungry they are, how many deer are in the area, and what else is available. Young deer sample things older deer have learned to avoid. In a hard winter or a drought summer, deer will eat plants they normally ignore.

"Deer-resistant" means a plant is rarely damaged in most situations. Think of it as better odds, not a guarantee.

What deer tend to avoid

  • Strong scents: aromatic foliage like catmint, Russian sage, lavender, salvia, oregano, thyme, rosemary and bee balm
  • Fuzzy or rough leaves: lamb's ear, yarrow, dusty miller, ageratum
  • Toxic or bitter compounds: daffodils, bleeding heart, foxglove, hellebore, euphorbia (spurge), boxwood
  • Tough, grassy or leathery textures: ornamental grasses, yucca, ferns

What deer tend to love

  • Hostas, daylilies, tulips and lilies
  • Garden phlox and impatiens
  • Roses and hydrangeas
  • American arborvitae and yews (in winter)
  • Tender new growth on young trees and shrubs
  • Most vegetables, especially beans, peas, lettuce, sweet potatoes and sweet corn

Deer-resistant perennials for shade

  • Hellebore: evergreen leaves, late-winter flowers, and toxic, so deer leave it.
  • Bleeding heart: spring hearts on arching stems.
  • Ferns: Japanese painted fern, ostrich fern and others are rarely touched.
  • Lungwort and brunnera: spotted and silver leaves, blue spring flowers.
  • Astilbe: feathery plumes, generally avoided.
  • Dead nettle (lamium): silvery groundcover for dry shade.
  • Japanese forest grass: a grass for shade.
  • Variegated Solomon's seal: arching stems and white bells.

If you love hostas but have deer, try planting them close to the house, where deer are less comfortable, and protect them with repellents in spring.

Deer-resistant shrubs and trees

  • Boxwood: rarely touched.
  • Juniper and spruce: prickly needles discourage browsing.
  • Smokebush: usually avoided.
  • Spirea and forsythia: generally left alone.
  • Lilac: mature shrubs usually escape serious damage.
  • Pieris: evergreen and toxic, so deer avoid it.
  • Weigela and potentilla: usually left alone.
  • Ornamental grasses: feather reed grass, switchgrass, maiden grass and fountain grass.

Arborvitae warning: American arborvitae is a winter favorite of deer, which can strip hedges bare up to about 5 feet. In deer country, consider Green Giant arborvitae (less palatable), spruces or junipers for screening.

Deer-resistant annuals and herbs

  • Annuals: marigolds, ageratum, snapdragons, dusty miller, annual salvia, lantana, cleome, angelonia, sweet alyssum and euphorbia Diamond Frost.
  • Herbs: almost all strongly aromatic herbs, including rosemary, sage, thyme, oregano, mint, chives and lavender.
  • Bulbs: daffodils and alliums are reliably avoided; tulips are deer candy.

Strategies that actually help

Plant choice is only part of the solution. In high-pressure areas, combine several of these.

1. Fencing

A physical barrier is the only truly reliable protection.

  • Tall fences. About 8 feet for an upright fence in open areas.
  • Double fences. Two shorter fences, about 4 to 5 feet tall and 3 to 5 feet apart. Deer can't judge a landing spot between them.
  • Small enclosures. Deer are reluctant to jump into small, enclosed spaces, so a fenced vegetable garden can be shorter than a fenced yard.
  • Netting and cages. Wrap young trees and shrubs in netting or cages through their first few winters. Use trunk guards to prevent buck rub damage on young tree trunks in fall.

2. Repellents

Repellents work by smell, taste or both. To get the most out of them:

  • Start early, before deer develop a habit of visiting.
  • Reapply after heavy rain and as new growth emerges. New leaves aren't protected.
  • Rotate products every month or two so deer don't get used to one.
  • Follow labels, especially on edibles.

We carry repellents and can help you choose. Ask at the counter.

3. Smart design

  • Put favorites close to the house, near entries and paths where deer feel exposed.
  • Surround vulnerable plants with resistant ones. A border of catmint, salvia and lavender around a bed of phlox won't stop a determined deer, but it can make the bed less inviting.
  • Use containers on a deck or porch for tulips, hostas and other favorites.
  • Plant less, protect more. A small, fenced vegetable garden often produces more than a big unprotected one.

4. Scare tactics

Motion-activated sprinklers, lights and noisemakers can work, especially when moved regularly. Deer get used to anything that never changes.

Protecting young trees and shrubs from bucks

Browsing isn't the only damage. In fall, bucks rub their antlers on the trunks of young trees, often small, smooth-barked trees one to four inches across. The rubbing strips the bark and can girdle and kill the tree. Protect young trunks from September through early winter with a cylinder of hardware cloth or welded-wire fencing, or a few sturdy stakes around the trunk. Plastic tree guards help too, but remove them in spring so they don't trap moisture against the bark.

Fencing: what actually keeps deer out

If you garden where deer pressure is heavy, fencing is the only truly reliable solution.

  • Height matters. White-tailed deer easily jump a 6-foot fence, so 8 feet is the usual recommendation for a single vertical fence. A shorter fence works better if it's angled outward or doubled.
  • Two fences can beat one tall one. Two parallel fences 4 to 5 feet tall and about 4 to 5 feet apart confuse deer, which don't like jumping into a narrow space.
  • Fence the vegetable garden at least. If you can't fence the whole yard, protect the plants deer love most: vegetables, hostas, roses and young fruit trees.
  • Netting draped over shrubs in winter is a cheap, temporary fix for arborvitae and yews.

Repellents: how to use them so they work

  • Start before damage begins. Deer form feeding habits. Apply repellents in early spring as new growth emerges, before they find your hostas.
  • Reapply on schedule, and after heavy rain. New growth isn't protected until it's sprayed.
  • Rotate products. Deer get used to one scent or taste. Switch between an egg-based repellent and a garlic, capsaicin or other type through the season.
  • Don't spray edibles with products not labeled for food crops. Read the label.

Deer and your vegetable garden

Deer eat most vegetables, especially beans, peas, lettuce, sweet potatoes and squash leaves. Onions, garlic, leeks and strongly scented herbs are usually left alone, and some gardeners edge vegetable beds with them. Honestly, though, the only thing that consistently works for a vegetable garden in deer country is a fence.

Keep a few notes about which plants get eaten in your own yard and when. Local deer have local tastes, and after one season you'll know which plants to protect and which ones they ignore.

Final thoughts

Gardening with deer is about tilting the odds: favor plants they tend to skip, protect the ones they love, and stay a step ahead with fencing or repellents. Every plant page in our catalog notes when a plant is generally considered deer resistant. You can also filter the catalog for "Deer resistant." And if deer are eating something we said they'd leave alone, let us know. Local experience is the best guide there is.

Frequently asked questions

Are any plants completely deer-proof?

No. Deer-resistant means deer usually avoid a plant, not that they never eat it. A hungry deer in late winter, or a young deer still learning what tastes good, will sample almost anything. Resistant plants just suffer far less damage.

What flowers do deer avoid in Indiana?

Strongly scented or fuzzy plants and those with toxic or bitter compounds: catmint, Russian sage, lavender, perennial salvia, yarrow, ornamental onion, lamb’s ear, bleeding heart, daffodils, hellebore, coreopsis, and ornamental grasses. Among annuals: marigolds, ageratum, snapdragons, dusty miller and most herbs.

What plants do deer love most?

Hostas, daylilies, tulips, garden phlox, roses, American arborvitae, yews, hydrangeas, impatiens, pansies, and tender vegetable crops like beans, lettuce and sweet potatoes are deer favorites.

How high does a deer fence need to be?

Around 8 feet for a single upright fence in open areas, since deer can jump high. Shorter fences can work if they’re angled outward, doubled (two fences a few feet apart), or around small spaces where deer can’t see a safe landing spot.

Do deer repellents work?

Many do, for a while. Odor- and taste-based repellents work best when applied before damage starts, reapplied after rain and new growth, and rotated between products so deer don’t get used to one.
