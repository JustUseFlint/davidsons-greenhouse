Hanging Basket Care: How to Keep Baskets Full and Blooming All Summer

Watering, feeding, trimming and siting, plus how to rescue a basket that’s gone leggy or crispy by July.

Hanging baskets go home from the greenhouse in May looking spectacular. By late July, many are thin, crispy, or all stems and no flowers. Is the basket the problem? Usually not. Baskets are an extreme growing environment: a lot of hungry roots in a small amount of soil, hanging in sun and wind, drying out from every side. Here's how to keep yours lush all season.

Start with the right basket for the spot

The biggest decision happens before you get home: matching the basket to the light where it'll hang.

Sun baskets (six or more hours of direct sun)

  • Petunias and calibrachoa (million bells)
  • Verbena, lantana, portulaca and scaevola
  • Geraniums
  • Mixed baskets with sweet potato vine as a spiller

Shade and part-shade baskets

  • Ferns, which love humidity and bright shade
  • Fuchsias, which want cool roots and shelter from hot afternoon sun
  • Begonias and New Guinea impatiens
  • Spider plants and wandering dude, for foliage

A sun basket hung under a deep porch roof will stretch and stop blooming. A fern hung in full afternoon sun will scorch. If you're unsure, tell us which way the porch faces and how many hours of sun it gets, and we'll steer you.

Our ribbon sizes

At the greenhouse, our garden guide sorts baskets by ribbon: Yellow Ribbon baskets are 6 to 8 inches (succulents or houseplants), Blue Ribbon and Red Ribbon are 10 inches (annual flowers, or ferns, begonias, fuchsias and similar), and White Ribbon are 12-inch specialty baskets. See hanging baskets for details. Bigger baskets hold more soil, so they dry out more slowly and are more forgiving in July.

Watering: the make-or-break habit

More baskets are lost to inconsistent watering than anything else. Small soil volume, wind on all sides, and roots packed in tight mean a basket can go from moist to bone-dry in a single hot afternoon.

How to tell when to water

  • The lift test. Reach up and lift the basket from below. A freshly watered basket is noticeably heavy. A dry one feels light. You'll learn the difference within a week.
  • The finger test. Push a finger into the soil. If the top inch is dry, water.
  • Don't wait for wilting. By the time petunias droop, they're stressed. Repeated wilting leads to yellow leaves, dropped buds and leggy growth.

How to water

  • Water until it drains. A quick splash wets the surface and runs off. Water slowly, let it soak in, then water again until it drips from the bottom.
  • Water in the morning. Plants go into the heat of the day hydrated, and leaves dry quickly, which reduces disease.
  • Expect daily watering in summer. In July and August, sun baskets often need water every day, and on very hot, windy days, twice.
  • Use a wand. A watering wand with a gentle shower head makes reaching baskets easier and won't blast soil out of the pot.

Rescuing a basket that dried out

If the soil has pulled away from the sides and water runs straight through, the potting mix has become water-repellent. Take the basket down and soak the whole pot in a tub or bucket of water for 30 minutes to an hour, until it stops bubbling. Let it drain, then hang it in shade for a day or two to recover before moving it back.

Feeding: baskets are hungry

Every time you water, some of the nutrients wash out the bottom of the basket. A basket watered daily for a month has very little left. Pale or yellowing leaves, especially on petunias and calibrachoa, are the classic sign.

  • Weekly liquid feeding. From late May on, use a water-soluble fertilizer once a week at the label rate, or at quarter strength every time you water.
  • Slow-release as a backup. Sprinkling a slow-release granular fertilizer on the soil surface early in the season gives a steady baseline, but in hot weather it usually isn't enough on its own.
  • Iron for yellow petunias and calibrachoa. If new leaves turn yellow with green veins while older leaves look fine, the plants may be short on iron. Fertilizers with chelated iron help.
  • Never feed a bone-dry basket. Water first, then fertilize, to avoid burning roots.

Grooming: deadheading and trimming

Some newer petunia and calibrachoa varieties are "self-cleaning," dropping their old flowers on their own. Many other basket plants bloom longer if you remove spent flowers before they set seed.

  • Pinch off faded flowers of geraniums, fuchsias and older petunia varieties, taking the little green seed pod behind the petals too.
  • Trim long stems. In midsummer, petunias and calibrachoa often get long, bare stems with flowers only at the tips. Cut those stems back by about a third, feed, and within two weeks you'll have dense new growth and a fresh round of blooms. Cutting back one side at a time keeps the basket presentable.
  • Remove yellow and dead leaves to keep air moving and reduce disease.

Siting and hanging tips

  • Turn baskets every week or so if one side gets more sun. Otherwise you'll have a full side and a bare side.
  • Mind the wind. Exposed baskets dry much faster. If a basket constantly dries out, a more sheltered hook may solve it.
  • Check the hook. A soaked 12-inch basket is heavy. Make sure hooks and brackets are rated for the weight.
  • Don't hang too high to water. If it's a chore to reach, it won't get watered.

Choosing a healthy basket at the greenhouse

A little looking before you buy pays off all summer:

  • Look at the foliage, not just the flowers. Deep green, dense leaves all the way to the rim mean healthy roots. Yellowing lower leaves can mean the basket has been short on water or food.
  • Check the undersides of leaves for aphids, whiteflies or webbing.
  • Peek at the soil line. Plants should be planted firmly, without big gaps or exposed roots.
  • Think about size and weight. A 12-inch basket holds noticeably more soil than a 10-inch one, which means more forgiveness on hot days, but it's heavy when wet. Make sure your hook can take it.
  • Buy for the season you're in. In early May, baskets have been grown warm in the greenhouse. If a cold night is in the forecast, hang them in a protected spot or bring them in until the weather settles.

Building your own basket

If you'd rather plant your own, a few rules help:

  1. Use fresh potting mix, never garden soil. Garden soil compacts and drains poorly in a container. A quality mix with some moisture-holding material is ideal for baskets.
  2. Plant more densely than you would in the ground. Baskets look best when they're full early. A 12-inch basket often holds five or six plants.
  3. Combine plants with the same needs. Mix sun lovers with sun lovers, and shade plants with shade plants. Combining a thirsty fuchsia with a drought-tolerant portulaca means one will be unhappy.
  4. Think thriller, filler, spiller. One upright center plant, a few mounding plants, and something that trails over the edge.
  5. Leave an inch of space between the soil and the rim so water has room to soak in.

Mid-season makeovers

By August, some baskets are simply tired. A few options:

  • Cut back hard and feed. Most petunias, calibrachoa and verbena respond with a strong second flush that carries into fall.
  • Tuck in fresh plants. Pull a dead or tired plant and replace it with a new one.
  • Switch for fall. In September, swap summer baskets for fall color: mums, pansies and ornamental kale.

Overwintering basket plants

Most basket annuals are treated as one-season plants in Indiana, but a few are worth saving:

  • Ferns (usually Boston-type ferns) can come indoors to a bright, cool, humid spot. Expect some leaf drop as they adjust.
  • Spider plants and wandering dude are houseplants anyway and make easy indoor plants.
  • Fuchsias and geraniums can be overwintered in a cool, bright room or kept dormant in a cool basement, then cut back and restarted in spring.

Bring any plants in before nights fall into the 40s, and check for insects first.

The short version

Choose the right basket for your light, water until it drains (daily in summer), feed every week, and don't be afraid to cut it back in July. Do those four things and your baskets will still look good at Labor Day.

Frequently asked questions

How often should I water a hanging basket?

Check daily. In hot July weather, a 10- or 12-inch basket in full sun often needs water every day, sometimes twice. In cool or cloudy weather it may go two or three days. Water when the top inch of soil feels dry or the basket feels light when you lift it from below, and water until it drains out the bottom.

Why is my hanging basket getting leggy?

Most often it’s a mix of running out of fertilizer, not enough sun, and the natural tendency of petunias and similar plants to stretch by midsummer. Trim stems back by about a third, feed with a water-soluble fertilizer, and give it a week or two. Fresh growth and blooms usually follow.

What is the best fertilizer for hanging baskets?

A water-soluble, balanced or bloom-formula fertilizer applied weekly (or at a dilute rate every watering) works well, often combined with a slow-release granular fertilizer worked into the top of the soil. Petunias and calibrachoa are heavy feeders and yellow quickly without it.

Can I save a hanging basket that dried out completely?

Often, yes. Soak the whole basket in a tub or bucket of water for 30 minutes to an hour until bubbles stop, let it drain, and move it to shade for a day or two. Trim off crispy stems. Most annuals bounce back within a week if the roots weren’t dry for too long.

Which hanging basket plants do well in shade?

Ferns, fuchsias, begonias, New Guinea impatiens, and foliage plants like spider plants and wandering dude do well in shade or morning sun. Petunias, calibrachoa, verbena, lantana and portulaca need at least six hours of direct sun to bloom well.
