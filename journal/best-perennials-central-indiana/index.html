The Best Perennials for Central Indiana: Tough, Beautiful and Long-Lived

Our short list of reliable perennials for sun, shade, clay and drought, and how to combine them into beds that look good from April to November.

Perennials are the backbone of a garden that gets better every year. Plant them once and they return each spring, filling out and blooming for years, sometimes decades. Our potted perennial list runs to hundreds of named varieties. This guide narrows that down to the perennials we'd plant first in a central Indiana garden, with advice on planning, planting and keeping them going.

What makes a perennial "good" here?

Central Indiana is in USDA zone 6a on the current map. We have cold winters with freeze-thaw cycles, wet springs, hot humid summers and often a dry spell in late summer. Many yards sit on heavy clay. The best perennials for us:

  • Are reliably hardy to at least zone 5, which gives a margin for hard winters.
  • Tolerate clay, or at least don't rot in it over winter.
  • Handle humidity without melting into mildew and leaf spot.
  • Stand up to dry spells once established.
  • Bloom for a long time, or have foliage that looks good when they're not in bloom.

Every plant page in our perennial catalog notes its hardiness and whether it's reliably winter-hardy in Crawfordsville.

Our short list for sun

These want six or more hours of direct sun.

Coneflower (Echinacea)

The classic native prairie perennial. Purple coneflower blooms from early summer into fall, feeds bees and butterflies, and leaves seed heads that goldfinches love. Newer colors (orange, red, yellow, doubles) are fun, but the straight species and its close selections tend to be the longest-lived. See coneflower.

Black-eyed Susan (Rudbeckia)

Goldsturm black-eyed Susan is nearly indestructible: golden daisies from July to September, clay tolerant and drought tolerant. It spreads into broad clumps. See black-eyed Susan.

Catmint (Nepeta)

A cloud of lavender-blue from late spring into fall, gray-green foliage, and deer and rabbits leave it alone. Shear it by half after the first flush and it blooms again. Walker's Low is the popular, larger selection; Cat's Pajamas is compact. See catmint.

Perennial salvia (Salvia nemorosa)

Dense spikes of violet-blue in May and June, with more if you cut spent spikes. May Night and Caradonna are reliable classics. See perennial salvia.

Russian sage

A silvery haze of lavender from midsummer to fall. It handles heat, drought and poor soil, and only flops if it gets too much shade or rich soil. See Russian sage.

Daylily (Hemerocallis)

Tough, adaptable and long-blooming, especially rebloomers like Stella de Oro and Ruby Stella. Note: all parts of daylilies are highly toxic to cats. See daylily.

Coreopsis

Cheerful yellow (and now pink, red and bicolor) daisies for months. Threadleaf types are tough and fine-textured. See coreopsis.

Sedum

Upright sedums like Autumn Joy bloom late, look good all winter, and attract bees in September. Low sedums make tough groundcovers for hot, dry spots. See sedum.

Our short list for shade

Shade gardens rely more on foliage texture and color, with flowers in spring.

Hosta

The foundation of the Indiana shade garden, from 4-inch miniatures to 30-inch giants, in blue, gold, green and variegated. Slugs, deer and rabbits are the main enemies. See hosta.

Coral bells (Heuchera)

Grown for leaves in lime, caramel, purple, red and near-black, with airy flower spikes hummingbirds visit. Give lighter-colored varieties shade from afternoon sun. See coral bells.

Ferns

Japanese painted fern brings silver and burgundy; ostrich fern brings height in moist shade; autumn fern and male ferns tolerate drier spots.

Astilbe

Feathery plumes in pink, red, white and lavender in early summer. Astilbe needs consistent moisture; it crisps in dry shade. See astilbe.

Hellebore

The first flowers of the year, blooming in late winter and early spring, with evergreen leaves and deer resistance. See hellebore.

Brunnera and lungwort

Spring forget-me-not-blue flowers, then silver-patterned leaves all season. Both are good companions for hostas and ferns.

Natives that pull their weight

Native perennials evolved with Indiana's climate and support native pollinators. Many on our list are native or native selections:

  • Butterfly weed and swamp milkweed, host plants for monarch caterpillars
  • New England aster, the late-season pollinator magnet
  • Bee balm, beloved by hummingbirds
  • Liatris (blazing star), prairie spikes butterflies flock to
  • Goldenrod, a fall essential for bees (it doesn't cause hay fever; ragweed does)
  • Coneflower and black-eyed Susan
  • Columbine, Indian pink and cardinal flower for hummingbirds
  • Switchgrass, a native prairie grass for structure

For more, read our guide to pollinator gardens in Indiana.

Planning for a whole season of bloom

The trick to a border that's always doing something is planning for succession, so different plants peak at different times.

Plant in groups of three, five or seven of the same plant rather than one of everything. Drifts look intentional and read better from a distance. Repeat key plants through the bed to tie it together.

Planting perennials

  1. Prepare the bed. Loosen soil 8 to 12 inches deep and work in 2 to 3 inches of compost. In heavy clay, raising the bed a few inches helps plants that need drainage.
  2. Mind the spacing on each plant's tag or page. Perennials look sparse the first year, and that's normal. The old saying is "first year they sleep, second year they creep, third year they leap."
  3. Plant at the same depth as the pot. Exceptions: peonies (eyes no more than 2 inches deep) and bearded iris (rhizome tops at the surface).
  4. Water deeply after planting, and regularly through the first summer.
  5. Mulch with 2 inches of shredded mulch, keeping it off the crowns.

Ongoing care

  • Deadhead spent flowers on long bloomers like salvia, coreopsis, garden phlox and shasta daisy to keep them going. Leave the last flush of coneflowers and black-eyed Susans for birds.
  • Shear catmint and hardy geraniums after the first bloom for a tidy rebloom.
  • Pinch tall fall bloomers such as asters, sedum and mums in June for sturdier plants.
  • Stake early tall plants like delphinium, peony and hollyhock, before they need it.
  • Divide crowded clumps every three to five years.
  • Fertilize lightly, if at all. Most perennials grow too lush and floppy with heavy feeding.

Fall cleanup: less is more

Leave sturdy stems and seed heads standing over winter. They provide food for birds, shelter for overwintering insects, and winter interest. Cut back plants with diseased foliage (bee balm with mildew, peonies, iris). Do the rest of the cleanup in early spring, before new growth gets tall.

Problem plants and problem spots

  • Wet clay in winter kills lavender, penstemon, gaura, blanket flower and others that need drainage. Plant them in raised beds or on slopes.
  • Deer and rabbits love hostas, daylilies and phlox. See our deer-resistant plant guide.
  • Aggressive spreaders like snow-on-the-mountain, lamium and creeping Jenny can take over. Plant them where they can roam, or in containers.

Pets and perennials

Some popular perennials are toxic to pets. Lilies and daylilies are extremely dangerous to cats; lily of the valley, foxglove, monkshood and hellebore are toxic to many animals. Our plant pages note well-documented pet toxicity with a link to the ASPCA plant list.

Dividing perennials

Many perennials get crowded after three to five years. You'll notice fewer flowers, or a dead patch in the middle of the clump. Dividing renews the plant and gives you free plants for other beds.

  • When: divide spring and summer bloomers in early fall, and fall bloomers in spring. Hostas, daylilies and Siberian iris are forgiving almost any time the weather is cool and the soil is moist.
  • How: dig up the whole clump, then split it with a sharp spade or two garden forks back to back. Each piece needs healthy roots and several growing points.
  • Replant at the same depth, water deeply, and keep the soil moist for a few weeks.
  • Bearded iris are the exception: divide them in mid to late summer, about six weeks after bloom, and replant the rhizomes so their tops sit at the soil surface.
  • Some perennials prefer to be left alone. Peonies, baptisia, butterfly weed and other deep-rooted plants resent being moved. Plant them where they can stay.

Cutting back: fall or spring?

You don't have to cut everything down in fall. Leaving seed heads on coneflowers, black-eyed Susans and ornamental grasses feeds birds and shelters beneficial insects through winter, and the stems help catch snow that insulates the crowns. Cut those back in early spring before new growth starts. Do cut back and remove foliage of plants that had disease, such as peonies with leaf blotch or bee balm with heavy powdery mildew, so the problem doesn't overwinter in the bed.

Frequently asked questions

What perennials bloom all summer in Indiana?

Catmint, Russian sage, coreopsis, perennial salvia (with deadheading), reblooming daylilies like Stella de Oro, black-eyed Susan, coneflower and blanket flower all bloom for months. Combine early, mid and late bloomers for continuous color.

What perennials grow well in clay soil?

Black-eyed Susan, coneflower, daylily, switchgrass, swamp milkweed, bee balm, hosta, perennial sunflower and New England aster all tolerate Indiana clay. Avoid lavender, penstemon and other plants that need sharp drainage unless you raise the bed.

When should I plant perennials?

Spring and early fall are ideal. Container-grown perennials can be planted any time the ground isn’t frozen, with regular watering through the first summer.

How often should I divide perennials?

Many perennials benefit from dividing every three to five years, when clumps get crowded or the center dies out. Divide spring and summer bloomers in early fall or spring, and bearded iris in July or August. Some, like peonies and baptisia, are best left alone.

Should I cut back perennials in the fall?

It depends. Cut back plants with diseased foliage, like bee balm with mildew or peonies. Leave sturdy seed heads such as coneflower, black-eyed Susan, sedum and ornamental grasses for winter interest, birds and overwintering insects, then cut them down in early spring.
