The Central Indiana Planting Calendar: What to Plant, Month by Month

A month-by-month plan for the vegetable garden, flower beds and landscape, built around Montgomery County’s frost dates and soil temperatures.

Gardening in central Indiana is a game of timing. Our springs swing between shirt-sleeve afternoons and hard frosts, our summers turn hot and humid, and fall can hang on into November or quit in early October. Plant too early and a cold snap can set you back weeks. Plant too late and your tomatoes are just starting to ripen when the first frost arrives.

This calendar is how we think about the year at the greenhouse in Crawfordsville. It’s built around two numbers every Montgomery County gardener should know, the average last spring frost and the average first fall frost, plus the soil temperatures different crops need. Use it as a guide, not a rulebook. Watch the forecast, feel the soil, and adjust.

The numbers behind the calendar

Hardiness zone

Crawfordsville sits in USDA zone 6a on the 2023 hardiness map, which means an average coldest winter night between -10°F and -5°F. The previous map put much of central Indiana in zone 5b, and many plant tags and older references (including the herb notes in our own garden guide) still say "most of Indiana is zone 5." For perennials, shrubs and trees, choosing plants rated to zone 5 gives you a buffer for the occasional brutal winter. You can look up your exact address on the USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map.

Hardiness zones only tell you about winter cold. They say nothing about when to plant in spring, and that's what the rest of this calendar is about.

Frost dates

In central Indiana the average last spring frost falls in late April, and the average first fall frost arrives around mid-October. "Average" is doing a lot of work in those sentences: it means roughly half of years see a frost after that date. Low-lying gardens, valleys and spots along creeks can run colder than the rest of town. For long-term records at stations near you, the Midwestern Regional Climate Center at Purdue is the place to look.

That's why we tell customers that mid-May is the safe date for frost-tender plants such as tomatoes, peppers, basil, impatiens and most tropicals. It isn't that frost is likely in mid-May. It's that the cost of a late frost is high and the reward for planting early is small.

Soil temperature

Seeds and roots respond to soil temperature more than air temperature. A few useful thresholds:

Crop groupMinimum soil temp to plantNotes
Peas, spinach, lettuce, onionsAbout 40°FGerminate slowly in cold soil, but they do germinate
Beets, carrots, radishes, potatoesAbout 45–50°FSeed potatoes rot in cold, wet ground
Sweet corn, green beansAbout 55–60°FBeans rot easily in cold soil
Squash, cucumbers, melonsAbout 60–65°FTransplants sulk below this
Peppers, eggplant, okra, sweet potatoesAbout 65°F+The most heat-hungry crops

An inexpensive soil thermometer pushed 2 to 4 inches deep in the morning tells you more than any calendar. For crop-by-crop dates, Purdue Extension publishes a detailed Vegetable Planting Calendar that we recommend keeping on the fridge.

January and February: plan, order and start the slow ones

The greenhouse is green even in January. Houseplants, succulents and cacti are in full swing, and it's a good time to repot your own indoor plants before spring growth begins. Outdoors, the work is mostly planning.

  • Sketch your garden. Note where the sun falls, where water stands after rain, and what grew where last year so you can rotate crops. Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant and potatoes are all in the same family and share diseases, so try not to plant any of them where another grew in the past couple of years.
  • Get a soil test. Testing before you amend saves money. Purdue Extension’s Montgomery County office can point you to soil testing options.
  • Start the slowest seeds indoors. Onions from seed, leeks, and slow perennials and annuals like begonias and lisianthus need 10 to 12 weeks indoors. Most gardeners do better buying these as transplants.
  • Prune fruit trees and grapes on mild days in late winter while they're dormant. Hold off on spring-flowering shrubs such as lilac and forsythia, or you'll cut off this year's flowers.

March: the first seeds and the first plantings

March is when the season actually begins. The rule for any outdoor planting is simple: if the soil sticks together in a muddy ball when you squeeze it, wait. Working wet clay ruins its structure for the whole season.

Indoors

  • Start tomatoes and peppers six to eight weeks before your planned transplant date, so mid- to late March for a mid-May planting. Peppers are slower and can go a week or two earlier.
  • Start broccoli, cabbage and cauliflower early in the month for April transplanting.
  • Give seedlings strong light: a shop light a few inches above the trays beats a windowsill every time.

Outdoors

  • Plant peas, spinach, lettuce, radishes and onion sets as soon as the ground can be worked. A light frost won't hurt them.
  • Plant asparagus crowns and rhubarb, both perennials you'll harvest for years.
  • Pot up pansies and violas for the porch. They shrug off frost and cold rain.

April: cool-season crops and early perennials

April brings the spring rush. Days warm, soil dries, and the greenhouse fills with flats.

  • Early April: plant potatoes (cut seed potatoes into pieces with one or two eyes and let them dry a day first), plus transplants of cabbage, broccoli, kale, kohlrabi and lettuce. Onion plants and bare-root onion bundles go in now too.
  • Mid-April: sow carrots, beets, Swiss chard and turnips. Keep the seedbed moist. Carrots can take two or three weeks to come up.
  • Perennials, shrubs and trees can be planted as soon as they're available. Container-grown plants establish well in cool spring weather.
  • Wait on the tender stuff. Warm April afternoons are tempting, but late April frosts are common. If you buy tomatoes or annuals early, keep them in a garage or porch and bring them in on cold nights.

A note on hardening off. Plants raised indoors or in a greenhouse need a week or so of gradual exposure to sun and wind before planting out. Start with an hour or two in a sheltered spot and build up. Skipping this step is the most common reason transplants stall or scorch.

May: the big planting month

May is when most of the garden goes in. We split it into two halves.

Early May

  • Keep sowing lettuce and radishes every couple of weeks.
  • Plant hardy annuals such as snapdragons, dusty miller and alyssum, and cool-tolerant herbs like parsley, chives, thyme and sage.
  • Sow sweet corn once the soil reaches about 55°F, and plant it in blocks of at least four short rows rather than one long row so it pollinates well.

Mid- to late May

Once frost danger has passed, usually somewhere around the middle of the month:

  • Plant tomatoes, peppers, eggplant and tomatillos. Bury tomato stems deeper than they grew in the pot. They root along the buried stem.
  • Sow green beans, cucumbers, squash, melons and pumpkins, or set out transplants.
  • Plant basil (it hates cold nights), sweet potato slips, and okra.
  • Fill beds and containers with warm-season annuals: petunias, zinnias, marigolds, impatiens, begonias, vinca, lantana, coleus.
  • Hang hanging baskets outside for good and begin a regular watering routine.

June: fill gaps and stay ahead of weeds

  • Mulch vegetable beds and flower beds once the soil has warmed. Two to three inches of shredded mulch or straw holds moisture and smothers weed seedlings.
  • Succession-sow bush beans every two to three weeks for a steady harvest.
  • Plant pumpkins for Halloween between late May and mid-June. Most varieties need 100 to 120 days.
  • Pinch tall fall-blooming perennials such as asters, sedum and garden mums by about a third once or twice before early July. That gives sturdier, bushier plants.
  • Start a regular feeding routine for containers and baskets. Potting mix runs out of nutrients quickly in summer.

July and August: water, harvest and plan for fall

High summer is about keeping plants alive and productive.

  • Water deeply and less often. An inch of water a week, delivered in one or two soakings at the base of the plant, beats a daily sprinkle. Early morning is best.
  • Harvest often. Beans, cucumbers, zucchini and herbs produce more when you pick regularly.
  • Late July: start the fall garden. Sow or transplant broccoli, cabbage, kale and collards for autumn harvest; they often taste better after a light frost.
  • Mid-August: sow lettuce, spinach, radishes and turnips for fall. Afternoon shade helps seedlings through the heat.
  • Cut back tired annuals and refresh containers. Late summer is a good time for a container makeover.

September: the second spring

September is one of the best planting months of the year in Indiana, and it's often overlooked.

  • Plant trees, shrubs and perennials. Warm soil and cooler air mean strong root growth with less stress. Keep watering until the ground freezes.
  • Seed or overseed lawns in late August through September, when cool-season grasses germinate best.
  • Garden mums and ornamental cabbage and kale arrive for fall color.
  • Dig and divide crowded spring- and summer-blooming perennials such as daylilies, hostas and irises. Bearded iris prefers dividing in July or August.

October: bulbs, garlic and putting beds to rest

  • Plant garlic cloves a few weeks before the ground freezes, then mulch with straw.
  • Plant spring bulbs such as tulips, daffodils and alliums once the soil cools.
  • Harvest winter squash and pumpkins before a hard freeze, leaving a few inches of stem, and cure them in a warm, dry spot.
  • Dig sweet potatoes before frost and cure them somewhere warm for about ten days.
  • Bring in houseplants that summered outdoors before nights drop into the 40s. Check them for hitchhiking insects first.

November and December: protect, clean up and rest

  • Water evergreens and newly planted trees deeply before the ground freezes. Winter burn on arborvitae and boxwood often comes from dry roots, not just cold.
  • Leave seed heads on coneflowers, black-eyed Susans and grasses for birds and winter interest.
  • Clean, sharpen and oil tools.
  • Visit the greenhouse for Christmas trees, wreaths and poinsettias, and start next year's wish list.

The bottom line

Central Indiana rewards patience in spring and ambition in fall. Plant cool crops early, hold tender plants until mid-May, and treat September as a second planting season. Above all, garden by the weather, not by the date on a seed packet.

Frequently asked questions

When is it safe to plant tomatoes in Crawfordsville?

Most years, mid-May. The average last spring frost in central Indiana falls in late April, but tomatoes also need warm soil and nights that stay above about 50°F. Planting them into cold ground in late April rarely gets you an earlier harvest. Set them out once the weather has settled, usually the second or third week of May.

What can I plant in March in central Indiana?

Once the soil is dry enough to crumble in your hand, plant peas, spinach, onion sets, lettuce and radishes from seed, plus asparagus crowns and rhubarb. Indoors, March is the month to start tomatoes and peppers under lights. Pansies and violas can go into pots and beds as soon as the worst cold has passed.

Is it too late to plant in June?

No. June is a fine time for warm-season crops like beans, squash, cucumbers and sweet potatoes, and for heat-loving annuals. Container-grown perennials, shrubs and trees can be planted any time the ground isn’t frozen. Just plan on watering faithfully through the first summer.

What zone is Crawfordsville, Indiana?

On the 2023 USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map, Crawfordsville is in zone 6a, with an average coldest winter night of -10 to -5°F. Older references, including the herb notes in our 2025 Garden Guide, say most of Indiana is zone 5. That was true on the older map, and choosing plants hardy to zone 5 still gives you a margin of safety.

When should I plant garlic in Indiana?

In October, a few weeks before the ground freezes. Plant individual cloves pointed end up about 2 inches deep and 6 inches apart, then mulch with straw after the ground cools. You’ll harvest the next July.
