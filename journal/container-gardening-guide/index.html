Container Gardening Guide: Pots, Soil, Plants and Care for Porches and Patios

Choosing pots and potting mix, designing with thrillers, fillers and spillers, and the watering and feeding routine that keeps containers full from May to frost.

Containers put color and food right where you live: by the front door, on the patio, along the deck rail. They're also the easiest way to garden if you have poor soil, limited space, or just want a big impact without digging a bed. Here's how to choose the right pot and mix, design containers that look full and balanced, and keep them going all summer.

Step 1: Choose the container

Size matters most

Bigger pots are easier. They hold more soil, which means more water and nutrients and less temperature swing, so plants are less stressed and you water less often. For a mixed container that will last all season, 14 inches across or larger is a good starting point. Small pots look charming but can dry out twice a day in July.

We carry clay, glazed and plastic pots at the greenhouse. You can also bring your own containers for our custom container planting service.

Drainage is non-negotiable

Every container needs a hole in the bottom. Without one, water pools and roots rot. And despite the old advice, a layer of gravel or pot shards at the bottom doesn't help drainage. It actually raises the soggy zone higher in the pot. Use potting mix all the way down, with a piece of screen or a coffee filter over the hole if you want to keep mix from washing out.

Step 2: Use the right potting mix

Never use garden soil in containers. It compacts, drains poorly and suffocates roots. Use a quality potting mix: a light blend of peat or coir, bark, and perlite or vermiculite.

  • For most annuals and vegetables: a general potting mix, ideally with slow-release fertilizer included.
  • For succulents and cacti: a gritty, fast-draining cactus mix.
  • For big planters: you can fill the bottom third of very deep pots with an upturned plastic pot or empty plastic bottles to save mix and weight, as long as the plants' root zone has at least 10 to 12 inches of mix.

Refresh mix every season or two. Old potting mix breaks down, compacts and holds less air.

Step 3: Design with thriller, filler and spiller

The simplest formula for a great mixed container:

  • Thriller: one tall, bold centerpiece plant that gives height. Think cordyline or spike, purple fountain grass, canna, upright coleus, or an angelonia or salvia for flowers.
  • Filler: mounded plants that fill the middle. Geraniums, begonias, coleus, lantana, calibrachoa, angelonia, euphorbia Diamond Frost, dusty miller.
  • Spiller: trailing plants that soften the edge and drape over the rim. Sweet potato vine, licorice plant, bacopa, vinca vine, dichondra Silver Falls, creeping Jenny, trailing petunias.

In a pot viewed from all sides, put the thriller in the center. For a pot against a wall, put it toward the back.

Plant combinations that work

Full sun, hot and bright

  • Thriller: purple fountain grass
  • Fillers: orange lantana, yellow calibrachoa
  • Spiller: lime sweet potato vine

Full sun, cool and elegant

  • Thriller: white angelonia
  • Fillers: blue salvia, white euphorbia Diamond Frost
  • Spiller: silver licorice plant

Part shade

  • Thriller: upright coleus
  • Fillers: begonias, New Guinea impatiens
  • Spiller: creeping Jenny or vinca vine

Full shade

  • Thriller: a fern
  • Fillers: impatiens, caladiums
  • Spiller: variegated ivy

Pollinator pot

  • Thriller: agastache or salvia
  • Fillers: lantana, zinnias
  • Spiller: verbena

Color tips

  • Pick a palette of two or three colors plus foliage, rather than one of everything.
  • Use foliage. Silver, chartreuse and burgundy leaves keep a pot looking good even between flushes of bloom.
  • Repeat the same container design in a pair or trio for a polished look.

Step 4: Plant it

  1. Fill the pot with moist potting mix to a few inches below the rim.
  2. Arrange plants, still in their pots, to check spacing and look.
  3. Plant the thriller first, then fillers, then spillers around the edge, angling spillers slightly outward.
  4. Fill gaps with mix, firm gently, and leave about an inch between the soil surface and the rim so water has room to soak in.
  5. Water thoroughly, until it runs out the bottom.

Plant containers more densely than garden beds. They're meant to look full quickly. The trade-off is that a crowded pot needs more water and food.

Step 5: Watering

Containers dry out much faster than garden beds, especially small pots, terra-cotta, and pots in sun and wind.

  • Check daily in summer. Push a finger into the mix. If the top inch is dry, water.
  • Water thoroughly until water drains from the bottom. Light sprinkles only wet the top.
  • Water in the morning, so plants go into the heat hydrated.
  • Self-watering pots and drip irrigation on a timer make summer much easier, especially for vacations.
  • If a pot dries out completely, the mix can become water-repellent. Set the pot in a tub of water for 30 minutes to rehydrate it.

Step 6: Feeding

Every watering flushes nutrients out of the pot. By midsummer, unfed containers look pale and stop blooming.

  • Use slow-release fertilizer at planting for a baseline.
  • Add a water-soluble fertilizer every week or two from June onward. Heavy bloomers like petunias and calibrachoa benefit most.
  • Watch for yellowing between the veins of new leaves on petunias and calibrachoa, a sign of iron deficiency. Fertilizers with chelated iron help.

Step 7: Grooming through the season

  • Deadhead spent flowers on geraniums, zinnias and older petunias.
  • Pinch coleus flower spikes to keep the plant bushy and leafy.
  • Trim leggy trailing plants and sweet potato vine when they get too long or start to smother their neighbors.
  • Replace a plant that fails rather than living with a gap.
  • Turn pots that lean toward the sun.

Succulent containers

Succulents are ideal for hot, sunny spots and forgetful waterers. Use shallow, wide pots with drainage holes, gritty cactus mix, and water only when the mix is completely dry. Our greenhouse makes succulent dish gardens, teacup planters and living wreaths. See our succulents and cacti.

Switching for fall

When summer annuals fade in September, don't let pots sit empty. Swap in fall color:

  • Garden mums for instant fall color
  • Ornamental cabbage and kale, which deepen in color as nights cool and last into early winter
  • Pansies and violas, which bloom in fall and often again in spring
  • Ornamental grasses and heuchera for texture
  • Pumpkins and gourds tucked around the base

Winter care for containers

Terra-cotta and many glazed pots can crack when wet soil freezes and expands. Empty them in late fall and store them dry, or move them into a garage. Frost-resistant pots such as concrete, fiberglass and resin can stay out. Evergreen winter containers of spruce tips, branches and berries are a nice touch for the front porch, and we carry wreaths and greens at the holidays.

Reusing potting mix

You can reuse potting mix for annuals, but refresh it. Remove old roots, then mix in about one-third fresh potting mix and some compost or slow-release fertilizer. Don't reuse mix from plants that had root rot or soil-borne disease, and start tomatoes in fresh mix each year.

Container care at a glance

  • Big pots, drainage holes, fresh potting mix
  • Thriller + fillers + spillers, matched to your light
  • Water daily in summer, until it drains
  • Feed every week or two from June
  • Deadhead, pinch and trim
  • Swap for fall, store fragile pots for winter

Frequently asked questions

What is the best soil for containers?

A quality potting mix, never garden soil. Potting mix is light, drains well and holds air around roots. Garden soil compacts in a pot, drains poorly and can carry weeds and disease. Refresh or replace potting mix every year or two.

Do all containers need drainage holes?

Yes, for nearly all plants. Without a hole, water collects at the bottom and roots rot. If you love a pot without one, drill a hole, or use it as a decorative cachepot with a plastic nursery pot inside that you can lift out to drain. A layer of gravel at the bottom does not fix drainage; it actually raises the wet zone.

How often should I water container plants?

Check daily in summer. Small pots, terra-cotta, and pots in full sun and wind may need water every day in July. Water when the top inch of mix is dry, and water until it runs out the bottom.

How many plants should I put in one container?

More than you’d think for an instant full look. A 14- to 16-inch pot often holds one centerpiece plant, three to four fillers and two to three spillers. Plants in a crowded pot need more water and food, so plan to feed weekly.

Can I leave containers outside in an Indiana winter?

Terra-cotta and many glazed ceramic pots can crack when wet soil freezes and expands. Empty them and store them dry, or move them to a garage. Heavy concrete, resin, fiberglass and frost-proof pots can stay out.
