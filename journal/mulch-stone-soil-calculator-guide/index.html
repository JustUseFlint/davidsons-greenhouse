How Much Mulch, Stone or Topsoil Do I Need? A Practical Guide with Formulas

The simple formula for cubic yards, the right depth for beds, paths, drives and raised beds, and how to mulch without harming your plants.

Few things are more frustrating than running out of mulch with one bed left to go, or ending up with a mountain of extra stone in the driveway. The good news is that figuring out how much you need is simple math. This guide covers the formula, the right depth for every common job, bulk vs. bagged, and a few mistakes worth avoiding.

You can also use the quick calculator on our mulch, stone and soil page.

The formula

Bulk landscape materials are sold by the cubic yard. One cubic yard is 3 feet × 3 feet × 3 feet, or 27 cubic feet.

To figure out what you need:

  1. Measure the area in feet: length × width = square feet.
  2. Convert depth to feet: depth in inches ÷ 12.
  3. Multiply square feet × depth in feet = cubic feet.
  4. Divide by 27 to get cubic yards.

Cubic yards = (length ft × width ft × depth in ÷ 12) ÷ 27

Example: a front foundation bed

A bed 30 feet long and 5 feet deep, mulched 3 inches deep:

  • 30 × 5 = 150 square feet
  • 3 ÷ 12 = 0.25 feet
  • 150 × 0.25 = 37.5 cubic feet
  • 37.5 ÷ 27 = about 1.4 cubic yards

Round up a bit to cover uneven ground and settling, so plan on 1.5 yards.

Irregular shapes

  • Circles (like a ring around a tree): area = 3.14 × radius × radius. A ring 6 feet across has a 3-foot radius: 3.14 × 3 × 3 ≈ 28 square feet.
  • Curvy beds: break them into rough rectangles and circles, calculate each, and add them up. Or pace it out and estimate generously.

The right depth for each job

Mulch in planting beds: 2–3 inches

That's enough to suppress weeds, hold moisture and moderate soil temperature without smothering roots. If there's still some old mulch in the bed, add only enough to bring the total back to 2 to 3 inches. Mulch piled deeper every year builds up into a thick mat that sheds water and starves roots of air.

Mulch around trees: 2–3 inches, wide, and never against the trunk

Spread mulch in a wide ring, at least 3 feet across and ideally out toward the drip line. Keep it a few inches away from the trunk. The "mulch volcano," a cone of mulch piled up against the bark, holds moisture against the trunk, invites rot and insects, gives rodents a place to chew, and can lead to roots circling up into the mulch. It's one of the most common reasons landscape trees decline.

Vegetable and annual beds: 2–3 inches of organic mulch

Straw (not hay, which is full of seeds), shredded leaves or fine shredded mulch, applied once the soil has warmed in late spring.

Decorative stone and river rock: 2–3 inches

Enough to cover the ground evenly. Larger stone needs a deeper layer. Landscape fabric underneath slows weeds and keeps stone from sinking into soil, but avoid fabric under organic mulch in planting beds, where it eventually traps a layer of dirt and weed seeds on top.

Gravel paths: about 3–4 inches

For a firm path, excavate a few inches, lay fabric, and fill with compactable gravel, adding a top layer of decorative stone if you like.

Driveway gravel: typically 4 inches or more

For new drives, a deeper base of larger gravel topped with a finer layer works best. Refreshing an existing drive usually takes a 2-inch top-up. Ask us about which stone suits your job.

Topsoil for leveling and new lawns: 4–6 inches

For a new lawn on poor ground, 4 to 6 inches of topsoil worked into the existing soil gives grass a better start. For filling low spots, add soil gradually, no more than an inch or two at a time over existing grass, so it can grow through.

Raised beds: the full depth

A raised bed needs to be filled completely, typically 8 to 12 inches. Use a blend of quality topsoil and compost, and expect it to settle a couple of inches in the first season.

Choosing a mulch

  • Shredded hardwood: knits together, stays put on slopes, breaks down into the soil over a couple of seasons.
  • Colored mulch (black, brown or red): holds its color longer. A good choice for front-yard curb appeal.
  • Straw and shredded leaves: best for vegetable gardens, where you'll turn them into the soil.
  • Stone: permanent and doesn't break down, but it heats up in summer and adds nothing to the soil. It's best for paths, drainage areas and around foundations, and less ideal around most plants.

Mulching mistakes to avoid

  1. Too deep. More than 3 to 4 inches can suffocate roots.
  2. Piled against stems and trunks. Leave a gap.
  3. Mulching cold, wet soil too early in spring. It keeps the soil cold. Wait until it warms in May for vegetable gardens.
  4. Mulching over weeds. Pull or smother existing weeds first; mulch prevents new ones better than it kills established ones.
  5. Layering mulch over mulch every year. Rake old mulch to loosen it and add only what's needed.
  6. Fabric under organic mulch in planting beds. It blocks the mulch from feeding the soil.

When to mulch

  • Spring (late April through May): the classic time, after the soil warms, to refresh beds.
  • After planting: mulch new trees, shrubs and perennials right away.
  • Late fall: a fresh layer around perennials after the ground cools reduces frost heaving.

Edging: the detail that makes mulch look finished

A clean edge keeps mulch in the bed and grass out of it. The simplest method is a hand-cut edge: use a flat spade to cut a vertical edge 3 to 4 inches deep along the lawn side, sloping back toward the bed, and remove the wedge of soil. The little trench holds mulch in place. Recut it each spring. For a permanent edge, metal, stone or brick edging set into the soil keeps grass from creeping in, which helps along curving beds and walks.

Topsoil, compost and potting mix: which is which?

  • Topsoil is mineral soil for filling, grading and building up beds. Quality varies; good topsoil is crumbly, dark and free of debris.
  • Compost is decomposed organic matter. It's an amendment, not a growing medium on its own. Work an inch or two into beds each year to improve structure and feed soil life.
  • Potting mix is a light, soilless blend for containers. Don't use garden soil or topsoil in pots; it compacts and drains poorly.
  • Raised bed mix is usually topsoil blended with compost, sometimes with other amendments. It's heavier than potting mix but lighter and richer than plain topsoil.

Stone: choosing size and type

  • Pea gravel: small and rounded, comfortable underfoot for paths and patios, but it shifts and migrates without edging.
  • River rock: smooth, rounded and decorative, in sizes from about 1 inch up. Good for dry creek beds, drainage swales and around downspouts.
  • Crushed limestone: angular pieces lock together, making it firmer for paths and driveways. Fines (the dust) pack down into a hard surface.
  • Boulders: use sparingly as accents, and bury them about a third of the way so they look natural rather than set on top of the ground.

Delivery and dumping tips

If you're having bulk material delivered, decide in advance where the pile should go. A driveway works well; lay a tarp first to make cleanup easier. Keep piles off the lawn if you can, since a heavy pile left more than a few days can kill the grass beneath it. A wheelbarrow and a wide shovel or a manure fork for mulch make spreading go much faster.

How long does mulch last?

Shredded hardwood usually breaks down noticeably within a year or two. Colored mulch keeps its color for about a season before fading. Expect to top up beds lightly every spring, adding only enough to restore a 2- to 3-inch layer.

Bringing it all together

Measure your beds, decide on a depth, run the numbers, round up 10 percent, and you'll have the right amount. Frequently asked questions

How much area does a cubic yard of mulch cover?

One cubic yard covers about 324 square feet at 1 inch deep, about 162 square feet at 2 inches, about 108 square feet at 3 inches, and about 81 square feet at 4 inches.

How deep should mulch be?

Two to three inches in most beds. Less won’t suppress weeds well; more can starve roots of oxygen and keep soil too wet. Keep mulch a few inches away from tree trunks and shrub stems.

How many bags of mulch equal a cubic yard?

A cubic yard is 27 cubic feet, so it takes about 13.5 two-cubic-foot bags, or 9 three-cubic-foot bags. For big jobs, bulk is usually more economical and less plastic.

How deep should river rock or decorative stone be?

About 2 to 3 inches for most decorative stone, laid over landscape fabric if you want to slow weeds. Larger stone needs a deeper layer to cover the ground evenly.

How much topsoil do I need for a raised bed?

Multiply length × width × depth in feet, then divide by 27 for cubic yards. A 4×8-foot bed that is 1 foot deep needs about 1.2 cubic yards. Plan for some settling.
