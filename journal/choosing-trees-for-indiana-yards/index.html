Choosing and Planting Trees for Indiana Yards: A Practical Guide

Right tree, right place: matching size and site, native choices, and the planting and watering steps that decide whether a tree thrives for fifty years.

A tree is the longest-lived thing most of us will ever plant. The right one, in the right spot, gives shade, beauty and wildlife value for generations. The wrong one ends up crowding the house, tangling with power lines, or declining slowly because it was planted too deep. This guide covers how to choose well and plant right in central Indiana.

Start with the site, not the tree

Before you fall for a tree on the lot, answer these questions about where it's going.

How much room is there, really?

Look up and around. Note overhead power lines, the roofline, the driveway, sidewalks, septic lines and property lines. Then compare against the tree's mature size, not its size in the pot. A red maple sold at 8 feet tall can reach 40 to 50 feet tall and 30 to 40 feet wide. Every plant page in our tree catalog lists mature height and spread.

A rough guide for distance from the house:

Mature tree sizeMinimum distance from buildings
Small (under 25 ft): redbud, dogwood, Japanese maple, magnolia10–15 ft
Medium (25–45 ft): river birch, black gum, crabapple20–25 ft
Large (over 45 ft): oaks, sugar maple, tulip poplar, sycamore30–40 ft or more

Under power lines, plant only small trees.

How much sun?

Most shade trees want full sun. Understory trees such as flowering dogwood and many Japanese maples prefer some shade, especially in the afternoon. Matching a tree to its preferred light cuts problems dramatically.

What's the soil like?

Much of central Indiana has heavy clay that drains slowly. Some trees love wet feet, including river birch, red maple, black gum, sycamore, dawn redwood and willows. Others, like white pine, many junipers and redbud, decline in soggy ground. If water stands where you want to plant after a rain, choose accordingly.

Soil pH matters too. Pin oak, river birch and some maples can turn yellow (chlorotic) in very alkaline soil. A soil test tells you where you stand.

What do you want the tree to do?

  • Shade for the house or patio: plant on the south or west side, far enough out that the canopy shades the roof without crowding it.
  • Spring flowers: redbud, dogwood, magnolia, crabapple, flowering cherry.
  • Fall color: red and sugar maples, black gum, sweetgum, oaks.
  • Privacy and wind protection: evergreens such as spruce, white pine and arborvitae.
  • Wildlife: native oaks, which support an enormous number of insects (and therefore birds), plus fruiting trees like crabapple and persimmon.

Native trees worth considering

Native trees evolved with our climate and soils and support local wildlife. Several on our list are native to Indiana:

  • Red maple grows fast, colors red in fall, and handles wet sites. October Glory and Red Sunset are reliable selections.
  • Sugar maple is long-lived, with brilliant orange and gold fall color. It dislikes road salt and compacted soil.
  • Eastern redbud puts magenta flowers right on bare branches in April. A small tree, good near the house.
  • Flowering dogwood is a woodland-edge tree that wants some shade and good drainage.
  • River birch has peeling, cinnamon-colored bark, handles wet soil, and resists the bronze birch borer that kills white birches.
  • Oaks are slow to start and magnificent at maturity, and some of the best trees for wildlife.
  • Black gum has arguably the best scarlet fall color of any native tree.
  • Tulip poplar, Indiana's state tree, is tall, straight and fast, for big yards.
  • American sycamore is huge and fast, with white mottled bark. For big spaces only.
  • American persimmon bears sweet fruit after it softens in fall.

Trees to avoid

Some once-popular landscape trees have turned out to be invasive in Indiana. The best known is the Callery pear (Bradford and related pears), which spreads into fields and woodlands. Purdue Extension has written about how this once-popular landscape selection was found to be invasive. Indiana also prohibits the sale of a list of invasive plants, including tree-of-heaven, Japanese barberry, wintercreeper and several Asian bush honeysuckles. See the Indiana DNR invasive plant list. We leave invasive species off our list.

Choosing a healthy tree at the nursery

  • Look for a strong central leader (for most shade trees) and branches well spaced around the trunk.
  • Check the trunk for wounds, cracks or sunken areas.
  • Look at the root flare. You should be able to see where the trunk widens at the soil. If it's buried in the pot, you'll need to remove excess soil at planting.
  • Avoid trees with circling roots visible at the surface or poking out the drainage holes, if you have a choice.
  • Bigger isn't always better. Smaller trees establish faster and often catch up to larger ones within a few years.

How to plant a tree, step by step

Most tree failures trace back to planting. These steps make the difference.

  1. Call before you dig. Indiana 811 marks underground utilities for free. Call a few days ahead.
  2. Find the root flare. Remove the tree from its pot and gently scrape away soil from the top of the root ball until you find where the trunk flares out into roots. That flare must end up at or just above ground level.
  3. Dig wide, not deep. Make the hole two to three times as wide as the root ball, with sloping sides, and only as deep as the root ball is tall measured to the flare. Rough up the sides of the hole in clay so roots can penetrate.
  4. Deal with circling roots. Tease apart or cut roots that circle the outside of the root ball. Left alone, they can eventually strangle the tree.
  5. Set the tree. Lift by the root ball, not the trunk. Set it on undisturbed soil so it doesn't settle deeper. Check that it's straight from two directions.
  6. Backfill with native soil. Use the soil you dug out. Heavy amendments in the planting hole can create a "bathtub" effect in clay, where roots stay in the soft soil and water collects. Break up clods and firm the soil gently as you go.
  7. Water thoroughly as you fill, to settle the soil and remove air pockets.
  8. Mulch. Spread 2 to 3 inches of shredded mulch in a wide ring, at least 3 feet across and wider is better. Keep mulch a few inches away from the trunk. Never pile it against the bark in a "mulch volcano," which invites rot and rodents.
  9. Skip the fertilizer at planting time. Let roots establish first.
  10. Stake only if needed. Most trees do better unstaked. If the site is windy or the tree is top-heavy, stake loosely for one year only.

Watering: the first two years decide everything

A newly planted tree has only the roots it came with. Until those roots grow out into the surrounding soil, it depends on you.

  • Water deeply, about once or twice a week through the first two growing seasons. Let a hose trickle at the base for several minutes, or use a 5-gallon bucket with a few small holes drilled in the bottom.
  • Check before you water. Dig down a few inches near the root ball. If it's moist, wait. Overwatering in clay drowns roots.
  • Keep watering in fall. Water until the ground freezes, especially evergreens, which lose moisture through their needles all winter.
  • Watch in drought. Even established trees benefit from deep watering in long dry spells.

Pruning young trees

Light structural pruning in the first few years sets up a strong tree for life.

  • Wait a year after planting, except to remove broken or dead branches.
  • Prune in late winter while trees are dormant. Oaks especially should be pruned only in winter to reduce the risk of oak wilt.
  • Keep one leader on shade trees. Remove competing upright stems.
  • Remove crossing and rubbing branches, and branches with narrow, tight crotches.
  • Never top a tree. Cutting back main branches to stubs creates weak, fast regrowth and decay.

Planting fruit trees

Fruit trees follow the same planting rules, with a few extras:

  • Full sun is non-negotiable.
  • Check pollination. Many apples, pears, sweet cherries and some others need a second compatible variety nearby. Our garden guide marks each fruit tree as needing a pollinator (P) or self-fertile (S), and so do our fruit tree pages.
  • Plan for spraying and pruning. Fruit trees need more care than shade trees. Tart cherries, pears and some disease-resistant apples are the most forgiving.

Evergreens for screens and windbreaks

Spruce, white pine and arborvitae are the workhorses of Indiana privacy screens. A few tips:

  • Space for mature width. It's tempting to plant close for instant privacy, but crowded evergreens shade each other out from the bottom.
  • Stagger rows for a denser screen.
  • Deer love American arborvitae. In deer country, consider Green Giant arborvitae, spruce or other less palatable choices.
  • Water before winter. Brown, burned needles in spring usually come from winter drought.

Common tree-planting mistakes

  • Planting too deep. The root flare should sit at or slightly above grade. A buried flare is one of the most common reasons young trees decline years later.
  • Leaving wire baskets, twine and burlap tight around the trunk. Cut away twine and pull back burlap from the top of the root ball once the tree is set.
  • Amending only the planting hole. Roots stay in rich, fluffy backfill and circle instead of spreading into native soil. Backfill with the soil you dug out.
  • Staking too tight or too long. Most trees don't need staking. If yours does, use loose, wide straps and remove them after one growing season.

Frequently asked questions

When is the best time to plant trees in Indiana?

Fall (September into October) and spring (April into May) are best. Cool air and warm soil in fall encourage root growth with less stress. Container-grown trees can be planted through summer too, as long as you water faithfully.

How deep should I plant a tree?

So the root flare, where the trunk widens into roots, sits at or slightly above the soil surface. Planting too deep is one of the most common causes of slow decline. Dig the hole two to three times as wide as the root ball but no deeper than the root ball is tall.

How much should I water a new tree?

Deeply, about once or twice a week during the first two growing seasons, more in hot, dry weather. A slow soak with a hose at the base for several minutes, or a 5-gallon bucket with small holes, works well. Check soil moisture a few inches down before watering again.

What are good native trees for central Indiana?

Red maple, sugar maple, eastern redbud, flowering dogwood, river birch, red oak, black gum, tulip poplar, American sycamore and American persimmon are all native to Indiana and available on our list.

Should I stake a newly planted tree?

Usually not. Most container trees establish better without stakes because some movement strengthens the trunk. Stake only in very windy sites or for top-heavy trees, loosely, and remove stakes after one year.
