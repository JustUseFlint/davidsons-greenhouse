Growing Tomatoes in Indiana: Varieties, Planting, Staking and Disease Control

Choosing varieties, reading disease-resistance codes, planting deep, and keeping blight and blossom-end rot in check through an Indiana summer.

No vegetable gets more attention in an Indiana backyard than the tomato, and none generates more questions at the greenhouse. The good news: tomatoes are very forgiving once you understand a few things about how they grow. This guide covers choosing varieties, planting, support, watering and feeding, and the diseases that show up nearly every Indiana summer.

Choosing the right tomatoes

Our garden guide lists dozens of tomatoes, from 1-ounce cherries to 2-pound heirloom beefsteaks. Three questions narrow the field quickly.

1. Determinate or indeterminate?

  • Determinate (D), or "bush," tomatoes grow to a set height (often 3 to 4 feet), then stop. Most of the fruit ripens over four to six weeks. They're ideal for canning, smaller spaces and containers. Celebrity, Roma, Health Kick and LaRoma III are determinate.
  • Indeterminate (I) tomatoes keep growing and producing until frost, often reaching 6 feet or more. They need tall stakes or cages but give a steady harvest all season. Most heirlooms and cherries are indeterminate, including Brandywine, Cherokee Purple, Sun Sugar and Supersweet 100.
  • Indeterminate short internode (ISI) varieties are a newer group that stays more compact but keeps producing.

When to plant

Tomatoes are frost-tender and grow poorly in cold soil. In Crawfordsville, plant around mid-May, once nights stay above about 50°F. See our guide to frost dates in central Indiana for more on timing.

If you grow your own from seed, start them indoors six to eight weeks before planting out, and harden them off for a week before they go in the ground.

How to plant tomatoes

Tomatoes have a superpower: they grow roots from any part of the stem that's buried. Use it.

  1. Choose a site with eight or more hours of sun, and don't plant where tomatoes, peppers, eggplant or potatoes grew in the last two or three years.
  2. Water the plants about half an hour before transplanting. Our garden guide suggests using a full-strength solution of water-soluble fertilizer.
  3. Plant on a cloudy, calm day or in late afternoon so transplants don't wilt in the sun.
  4. Plant deep. Remove the lower leaves and bury the stem up to the first set of leaves. For a leggy plant, dig a shallow trench and lay the stem sideways, then gently bend the top upright. It will root along the whole buried length.
  5. Fill and firm. Fill the hole to within a few inches of the top, water thoroughly to settle the soil and remove air pockets, then finish filling and firm around the stem.
  6. Space them properly, 24 to 36 inches apart. Crowded tomatoes stay wet and get sick.

Support: stakes, cages or trellises

Get supports in place at planting time so you don't damage roots later.

  • Cages are simplest. Choose sturdy ones; the lightweight cones sold in many stores are too small for indeterminate varieties. Determinate tomatoes do fine in standard cages.
  • Stakes of wood or metal, 6 to 8 feet tall and driven a foot into the ground, work well for indeterminates. Tie the main stem loosely with soft ties every foot or so.
  • The Florida weave, twine woven between stakes along a row, is efficient for longer rows.

We carry tomato cages, stakes and plant supports at the greenhouse.

To prune or not

On staked indeterminates, pinching out some of the suckers (the shoots that form where a leaf meets the stem) keeps plants to one or two main stems, improves air flow and makes tying easier. In cages, you can leave most suckers for more fruit. Determinate tomatoes shouldn't be pruned much, because it reduces the crop.

Do remove the lowest leaves, up to about a foot off the ground, once plants are established. That keeps soil from splashing onto leaves, which is how many diseases start.

Watering and mulching

Consistent moisture is the key to good tomatoes. Uneven watering, dry then soaked, causes cracked fruit and blossom-end rot.

  • Water deeply at the base of the plant, about 1 to 1.5 inches a week including rain. In midsummer heat, plants in sandy soil or containers need more.
  • Water in the morning and avoid wetting the leaves.
  • Mulch with 2 to 3 inches of straw or shredded leaves once the soil has warmed. Mulch evens out soil moisture and keeps disease spores in the soil from splashing up.

Feeding

Tomatoes are moderate feeders. If you've worked compost into the soil, a balanced fertilizer at planting and a side-dressing when the first fruits are the size of a golf ball is usually plenty. Too much nitrogen means huge leafy plants with few tomatoes.

The usual Indiana tomato problems

Humid summers make central Indiana a tough place for tomato foliage. Here's what you're most likely to see.

Early blight and Septoria leaf spot

These fungal diseases start on the lowest leaves: brown spots (early blight shows concentric rings like a target; Septoria shows many small spots with light centers), surrounded by yellowing. The disease climbs the plant through summer.

  • Remove spotted lower leaves promptly, and keep them out of the compost.
  • Mulch, water at the base, and space plants for air flow.
  • Rotate where you plant tomatoes each year.
  • Choose varieties with EB tolerance.
  • Fungicides labeled for tomatoes can slow spread if started early. Ask us for options.

Blossom-end rot

A sunken, leathery, dark patch on the bottom (blossom end) of the fruit. It's caused by a calcium shortage in the developing fruit, usually from uneven watering rather than a lack of calcium in the soil. The first fruits are most often affected. Water consistently and mulch. Our garden guide notes that a soil test, lime when the test calls for it, and calcium foliar sprays such as Yield Booster can also help.

Cracking

Splits around the top of the fruit, usually after heavy rain following dry weather. Even watering helps. Crack-resistant varieties include Big Beef, Celebrity and Park's Whopper.

Wilts

Plants that wilt in the heat of the day and don't recover by evening, sometimes on one side, may have Fusarium or Verticillium wilt. There's no cure for an infected plant. Choose V and F resistant varieties and rotate.

Tomato hornworms

Big green caterpillars, up to 4 inches long, that can strip a plant in days. Look for dark droppings on the leaves below, then hand-pick. If you find one covered in small white cocoons, leave it: those are parasitic wasps that will control others.

Blossom drop

Flowers fall off without setting fruit, usually during heat waves with days above the low 90s and warm nights, or during cold snaps. Fruit set resumes when temperatures moderate.

When in doubt, get a diagnosis before treating. Bring a sample to the greenhouse, or use the Purdue Plant & Pest Diagnostic Lab.

Harvesting

Pick tomatoes when they're fully colored and give slightly to a gentle squeeze. Tomatoes picked at the first blush of color will finish ripening on the counter with excellent flavor, which also keeps them away from birds and squirrels. Never refrigerate ripe tomatoes; it dulls their flavor and texture.

At the end of the season, before the first frost (usually mid-October), harvest any fruit that's showing color and bring it indoors to ripen.

Tomatoes in containers

No garden? Tomatoes grow well in pots of at least 5 gallons, and bigger is better. Choose determinate or compact types such as Sweet ’N’ Neat, or a Tumbling Tomato hanging basket. Use fresh potting mix, never garden soil. Water daily in summer and feed every week or two, because nutrients wash out quickly.

Quick checklist

  • Choose varieties for your purpose and disease history
  • Plant in mid-May, deep, 2 to 3 feet apart, in full sun
  • Put stakes or cages in at planting
  • Water deeply and evenly, and mulch
  • Remove lower leaves and spotted foliage
  • Rotate planting spots every year

Frequently asked questions

When should I plant tomatoes in central Indiana?

Around mid-May, once frost danger has passed and nights stay above about 50°F. Tomatoes planted earlier into cold soil usually just sit there and rarely produce sooner.

What do the letters after tomato names mean?

They’re disease-resistance codes. V is Verticillium wilt, F is Fusarium wilt (FF and FFF mean resistance to more races), N is root-knot nematodes, T is tobacco mosaic virus, A is Alternaria stem canker, St is Stemphylium gray leaf spot, TSWV is tomato spotted wilt virus, and EB and LB are early and late blight. More letters means more built-in protection.

Why are the bottoms of my tomatoes turning black?

That’s blossom-end rot, a calcium problem in the developing fruit that is usually caused by uneven watering rather than a lack of calcium in the soil. Keep soil evenly moist with deep, regular watering and mulch. It usually affects the first fruits and improves as the season goes on.

Why are my tomato leaves turning yellow with brown spots from the bottom up?

Most often that’s early blight or Septoria leaf spot, two fungal diseases common in humid Indiana summers. Remove affected lower leaves, mulch to stop soil splashing, water at the base, and give plants room for air flow. Choose resistant varieties next year and rotate where you plant.

Should I prune tomato suckers?

On staked indeterminate tomatoes, removing some suckers keeps plants manageable and improves air flow. In cages, you can leave most of them for a bigger crop. Don’t prune determinate tomatoes much; they set a fixed amount of fruit, and pruning reduces it.
