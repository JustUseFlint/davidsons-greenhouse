Last Frost Dates in Central Indiana: How to Read Them and When to Plant

What “average last frost” really means, why we wait until mid-May for tomatoes, and how to save your plants when a cold night sneaks up.

Every spring someone asks us the same question: is it safe to plant yet? It's the right question. The trouble is that the usual answer, "after your last frost date," is more slippery than it sounds. This guide explains what frost dates mean, why we lean conservative in central Indiana, and what to do when a late cold snap shows up in the forecast.

What a "last frost date" actually means

Frost dates come from decades of weather records. The average last spring frost is the date after which, historically, about half of years have had no more frost. Put another way, on the average date you have roughly a coin-flip chance of another frost still to come.

That's why you'll see frost dates expressed as probabilities in climate data, for example the date after which there's only a 10 percent chance of frost. For a Crawfordsville garden:

  • Average last spring frost: late April.
  • Safe planting for tender plants: around mid-May. By then the odds of frost are low, and the soil has warmed enough for heat-loving crops to grow instead of just sitting there.
  • Average first fall frost: around mid-October, varying by weeks from year to year.

If you want to look up the records for weather stations near you, the Midwestern Regional Climate Center at Purdue maintains decades of station data for Indiana.

Frost dates vs. hardiness zones

These are often confused. Your hardiness zone (Crawfordsville is 6a on the 2023 USDA map) tells you how cold winter gets on average, which matters for whether a perennial, shrub or tree survives the winter. Your frost dates tell you how long the growing season is, which matters for when to plant annuals and vegetables. A plant can be perfectly hardy in your zone and still have its new spring growth burned by a late frost. Hydrangeas and Japanese maples are both prone to this.

Why your yard may be colder than the forecast

Frost forms on clear, calm nights when heat radiates from the ground and plants into the sky. Several things make some gardens colder than others:

  • Low spots. Cold air is heavier than warm air and flows downhill like water. Gardens at the bottom of a slope or along a creek bottom can frost when the hilltop doesn't.
  • Open sky. Plants under a tree canopy or a porch roof lose heat more slowly than plants in the open.
  • Distance from buildings and pavement. Brick walls and driveways store daytime heat and release it at night. Beds against a south-facing wall are often a few degrees warmer.
  • Wind. A light breeze actually keeps frost from settling. The most dangerous nights are still and starry.

Pay attention to where frost shows up first on your lawn on cool fall mornings. Those are the spots to plant last in spring.

Sorting plants by frost tolerance

Not everything has to wait until May. We think of spring plants in three groups.

Frost-tolerant: plant from March into April

These handle light frosts and can go in as soon as the soil is workable:

  • Flowers: pansies, violas, snapdragons, sweet alyssum, dusty miller, ornamental cabbage and kale
  • Vegetables: peas, spinach, lettuce, radishes, onions, cabbage, broccoli, kale, kohlrabi, potatoes (planted as seed pieces), asparagus and rhubarb
  • Most hardy perennials, shrubs and trees, especially container-grown ones that have been kept outside

Tolerates cool, not frost: plant late April into early May

These won't die from a chilly night but can be damaged by frost:

  • Beets, carrots, Swiss chard and sweet corn (once soil reaches about 55°F)
  • Parsley, chives, thyme, sage and oregano
  • Geraniums and dahlias, with an eye on the forecast

Frost-tender: wait for mid-May

These are damaged or killed by frost, and many sulk in cool weather even without it:

  • Vegetables: tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, cucumbers, squash, melons, pumpkins, green beans, okra and sweet potatoes
  • Herbs: basil, lemongrass, stevia and other tropical herbs
  • Flowers: impatiens, begonias, coleus, vinca, lantana, zinnias, celosia and most tropicals like mandevilla and hibiscus
  • Houseplants that summer outdoors, which should wait until nights stay above about 55°F

Why we don't rush tomatoes

Tomatoes are the classic temptation. A warm week in late April comes along, and it seems silly to wait. But tomatoes planted into cold soil usually just stand still: roots barely grow below about 50°F, leaves may turn purple from cold stress, and a frost can kill them outright. A plant set out two weeks later into warm soil often catches up and passes the early one within a few weeks.

If you can't resist planting early, hedge your bets. Plant a few, keep a few in pots, and have frost cover ready.

How to protect plants from a late frost

When a frost warning pops up after you've planted, you have options.

  1. Water during the day. Moist soil holds and releases more heat than dry soil. Water beds in the morning or early afternoon before a cold night.
  2. Cover plants in the evening. Frost cloth (row cover), old bedsheets, cardboard boxes or upside-down buckets all work. Let fabric covers drape all the way to the ground. They work by trapping heat rising from the soil, not by insulating the plant on their own.
  3. Keep plastic off the leaves. Plastic sheeting conducts cold. If you use it, hold it off the plants on stakes and add a sheet underneath.
  4. Move containers. Pull pots and hanging baskets into a garage or against the house, or under a porch roof.
  5. Uncover in the morning once temperatures rise above freezing, so plants don't cook on a sunny day.

For tender transplants, protection is worth it for one or two cold nights. If the forecast calls for a hard freeze in the mid-20s, covers may not be enough for tender plants, and it's safer to bring anything you can indoors.

What frost damage looks like

The morning after a frost, damaged leaves look dark and water-soaked, then turn black or brown and papery over the next day or two. Don't rush to pull plants. Wait a few days. Many perennials and shrubs push new growth from undamaged buds. Tomatoes and basil rarely recover from a hard frost, but a light nip on the top leaves often grows out.

Hardening off: the step that makes the difference

Plants that went straight from a warm greenhouse to the garden are far more vulnerable to cold, wind and sun than ones that have adjusted. Over seven to ten days:

  • Days 1–2: an hour or two outdoors in shade and out of the wind
  • Days 3–5: a half day outside, with some morning sun
  • Days 6–8: most of the day outside, including some afternoon sun
  • Days 9–10: leave them out overnight if lows are above about 50°F for tender plants

Bring them in any night frost or a hard wind is forecast.

Fall frost: the other end of the season

The same rules apply in reverse in autumn. Around the first forecast frost in October:

  • Harvest ripe and nearly ripe tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, squash and basil. Green tomatoes that have started to blush will ripen on the counter.
  • Bring in houseplants and tropicals before nights drop into the 40s. Many tropicals are damaged by cold well above freezing.
  • Leave the frost-lovers. Kale, collards, Brussels sprouts, parsnips, carrots and leeks taste sweeter after a frost.
  • Plant garlic and spring bulbs a few weeks before the ground freezes.

Frost-proofing your planting plan

A few habits make late frosts far less stressful:

  • Stagger plantings. Rather than putting every tomato in on the first warm weekend, plant half in mid-May and half a week or two later. If a cold night hits the first batch, you have backups.
  • Buy early, plant later. Shopping early in May gets you the best selection, but there's no rule that plants have to go in the ground that day. Keep them in their pots in a sunny, sheltered spot and pull them into the garage on cold nights. Water daily, because small pots dry quickly.
  • Use containers for tender favorites. Pots of basil, peppers and patio tomatoes can be moved under cover in minutes.
  • Keep supplies ready. A roll of frost cloth, a few old sheets, clothespins, and some bricks or rocks to hold covers down.
  • Watch the dew point and the sky, not just the low. Clear skies, calm winds and a dry evening mean frost can form even when the forecast low is in the mid-30s.

Season extenders

If you want to push the season earlier or later, a little infrastructure goes a long way:

  • Row cover laid over hoops raises nighttime temperatures a few degrees and protects early crops from both frost and insects.
  • Cold frames, essentially bottomless boxes with clear lids, let you harden off transplants and grow lettuce and spinach weeks earlier in spring and later into fall. Open the lid on sunny days or plants will overheat.
  • Black plastic mulch warms the soil for heat-loving crops like melons, peppers and sweet potatoes.
  • Walls of water, rings of water-filled plastic tubes, store heat around individual tomato plants.

None of these make a tomato frost-proof, but they buy you a week or two at either end of the season.

The takeaway

Frost dates are averages, not promises. Plant cool-season flowers and vegetables early, hold tender plants until mid-May, and keep an old sheet by the back door until Memorial Day. Frequently asked questions

What is the average last frost date in Crawfordsville, Indiana?

Late April. Frost after that date is common, though, and low spots can see frost into early or even mid-May. That’s why we suggest mid-May for frost-tender plants like tomatoes, peppers, basil and impatiens.

What temperature kills tender plants?

Frost-tender plants are damaged when their leaves freeze, which can happen when the air is a few degrees above 32°F on clear, calm nights. A forecast low of 36°F or below is a good cue to cover or bring in tender plants. Basil and many tropicals are hurt by temperatures in the 40s even without frost.

Will covering plants protect them from frost?

Usually, for a light frost. Cover plants in the evening with frost cloth, old sheets or buckets, letting the cover reach the ground to trap heat from the soil, and remove it in the morning. Avoid plastic touching leaves. A hard freeze in the mid-20s can still damage tender plants under a cover.

Which flowers can I plant before the last frost?

Pansies, violas, snapdragons, sweet alyssum, dusty miller, ornamental cabbage and kale, and many perennials tolerate light frost. Hardened-off plants handle cold much better than ones straight from a warm greenhouse.

When is the first fall frost in central Indiana?

On average, around mid-October, though it varies by several weeks from year to year. Bring houseplants indoors, and harvest tomatoes, peppers and basil, before the first forecast frost.
