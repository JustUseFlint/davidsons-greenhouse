Growing Berries in Indiana: Strawberries, Blueberries, Raspberries and More

Which small fruits grow best in central Indiana backyards, how to fix soil for blueberries, and how to plant, prune and harvest each one.

Nothing beats a handful of berries warm from the sun. Better yet, small fruits produce within a year or two of planting and fit in ordinary backyards. Our garden guide lists strawberries, blueberries, raspberries, blackberries, elderberries, gooseberries, grapes, hardy kiwi and a cold-hardy fig. Here's how to choose and grow each in central Indiana.

Before you plant: site and soil

Nearly all berries need:

  • Full sun. Six hours minimum, eight or more for the best crops and sweetest fruit.
  • Good drainage. Standing water rots roots. On heavy clay, raised beds or raised rows help a lot.
  • Room to move around. Berries need picking, pruning and netting, so leave paths.
  • Distance from wild brambles, which can carry diseases into cultivated raspberries and blackberries.

Avoid Verticillium sites

Our garden guide (quoting Indiana Berry & Plant Co.) warns: don't plant strawberries where tomatoes, potatoes, peppers or eggplant have grown in the past four years. Those crops can carry Verticillium wilt, a soil fungus that attacks strawberries and raspberries.

Test your soil

A soil test tells you pH and nutrients. Most berries like a pH around 6 to 6.5. Blueberries are the big exception, needing pH 4.5 to 5.5.

Strawberries

Strawberries are the quickest berry, often fruiting the year after planting, or the same summer for day-neutral types.

Choosing types

  • June-bearing varieties give one large crop over two to three weeks in June. Our list: Earliglow (the earliest, very sweet), Honeoye (early, heavy yields) and Jewel (late, large and firm).
  • Day-neutral / everbearing varieties fruit from June until frost in smaller amounts. Our list: Albion and Seascape. Great for containers and small spaces.

Planting

  • Plant in early spring, about April, as soon as soil can be worked.
  • Set the crown at soil level, the point where roots meet leaves. Too deep and it rots; too shallow and it dries out.
  • Our garden guide's rule: no closer than 6 inches in any direction, in rows 10 to 12 inches wide. Overcrowding is the biggest problem in strawberry beds. It shrinks berries, increases disease and makes picking harder.

Care

  • First year, June-bearers: pinch off flowers so plants put energy into roots and runners. You'll get a much bigger crop next year.
  • Water about an inch a week, especially while fruit is forming.
  • Mulch with straw to keep berries clean and suppress weeds. That's where the name comes from.
  • Thin runners to keep the bed from becoming a mat.
  • Renovate June-bearers after harvest: mow off leaves, narrow the rows, thin plants and fertilize.
  • Rotate the bed after four or five years. Don't grow strawberries in the same spot for five or more consecutive years.

See strawberries.

Blueberries

Blueberries are beautiful shrubs as well as productive: white spring flowers, summer fruit and flaming red fall color. But they're particular about soil.

Fixing the pH

Most central Indiana soils are near neutral or alkaline, too high for blueberries. Options:

  1. Amend the planting area. Work in elemental sulfur based on a soil test, along with acidic organic matter such as peat moss. Sulfur takes months to lower pH, so ideally amend the fall before planting.
  2. Build raised beds filled with an acidic mix of peat, pine bark and soil.
  3. Grow in large containers, 15 gallons or more, using an acidic potting mix. This is often the most reliable approach in alkaline soil.

Yellowing leaves with green veins mean the pH is too high.

Choosing varieties

Plant at least two varieties that bloom together for better pollination and bigger berries. Mixing seasons stretches the harvest:

SeasonVarieties on our list
EarlyPatriot (more adaptable to wet soil)
Early–midBlue Jay, Blue Ray
Mid–lateChandler (very large)
LateElliott, Jersey, Legacy

Care

  • Mulch 3 to 4 inches deep with pine bark or wood chips. Roots are shallow.
  • Water consistently, since blueberries dislike drying out.
  • Remove flowers the first year or two so plants establish.
  • Prune in late winter from about year four: remove the oldest, least productive canes and weak twigs.
  • Net plants as berries color, or the birds will harvest first.

See blueberries.

Raspberries

Raspberries come in red, gold, black and purple, and in two fruiting habits.

  • Everbearing (fall-bearing): fruit on first-year canes in late summer and fall, and again lower down the next summer. Our list: Heritage (red, July and September), Anne (yellow), Fall Gold (golden, very sweet) and Nova (red).
  • Black raspberries: intensely flavored, summer-bearing. Our list: Bristol and Jewel.

Planting

Space plants 2 to 3 feet apart in rows, ideally with a simple trellis of posts and wires to keep canes upright.

Pruning, simplified

  • Everbearing reds and golds, easy method: in late winter, mow or cut every cane to the ground. You'll get one large, high-quality fall crop and fewer disease problems.
  • For two crops: after fall harvest, cut off only the fruited top portion of each cane; it fruits lower down the next summer. Then remove those canes after the summer crop.
  • Black raspberries: in summer, pinch new canes at 3 to 4 feet to encourage branching. After harvest, remove canes that fruited. In late winter, shorten side branches.

See raspberries.

Blackberries

Modern thornless and erect blackberries make picking painless.

  • Our list: Natchez (thornless, very large, early), Ouachita (erect, thornless, sweet) and Triple Crown (thornless, large).
  • Space 3 to 4 feet apart in rows, with a two-wire trellis.
  • Prune: canes fruit in their second year. After harvest, cut fruited canes to the ground. In summer, tip new canes at about 4 feet to encourage fruiting side branches.

See blackberries.

Grapes

Hardy American-type grapes thrive in Indiana. Our list includes Concord (seeded and seedless), Himrod (white seedless), Mars (blue seedless) and Reliance (red seedless, very hardy).

  • Plant in full sun with good air flow, 6 to 8 feet apart, on a sturdy two-wire trellis or arbor.
  • Prune hard every late winter. This surprises new growers: grapes fruit on one-year-old wood, and most of the vine should be removed each year, leaving a few canes or spurs.
  • Watch for Japanese beetles, black rot and birds.
  • Pet note: grapes and raisins are toxic to dogs.

See grapes.

Elderberries

American elderberry is a native shrub with large, flat clusters of white flowers in June and dark purple berries in late summer, used for jams, jellies, pies and syrups.

  • Our list: Adams, Bob Gordon, Johns, Nova, Ranch and York. Plant two varieties for better fruit set.
  • Elderberries tolerate moist soil and are great at the edge of a yard or in a wet spot.
  • Cook the berries. Raw berries, stems and leaves shouldn't be eaten.
  • Prune out canes older than three years in late winter.

See elderberry.

Gooseberries

Compact, cold-hardy and tolerant of a little shade, gooseberries give tart-sweet berries for pies and jam. Our list: Black Velvet, Captivator (fewer thorns), Hinnonmaki Red and Pixwell. Afternoon shade helps in hot summers, and good air flow reduces powdery mildew. See gooseberries.

Something different: hardy kiwi and Chicago Hardy fig

  • Hardy kiwi 'Issai' is a self-pollinating vine with grape-sized, smooth-skinned fruit you eat whole. It needs a strong arbor and protection from late spring frosts.
  • Chicago Hardy fig is the fig for the Midwest. If winter kills it to the ground, it regrows and fruits on new wood the same summer. Plant against a sunny south wall and mulch heavily, or grow it in a large pot moved into a garage for winter.

Rhubarb: the honorary berry

Not a berry, but it belongs next to the strawberries (strawberry-rhubarb pie, after all). Crimson Red rhubarb has large red stalks. Skip harvest the first year, eat only the stalks (the leaves are toxic), and pull stalks rather than cutting them. See rhubarb.

MonthTask
Late winterPrune grapes, blueberries, blackberries, raspberries, elderberries
AprilPlant strawberries, brambles, blueberries and grapes; mulch
MayNet strawberries; pinch first-year strawberry flowers
JuneStrawberry harvest; elderberry flowers
JulyBlueberries, black raspberries, blackberries, gooseberries; renovate strawberries
August–SeptemberEverbearing raspberries, elderberries, grapes, day-neutral strawberries
FallPlant fruit trees; amend soil for next spring's blueberries

Common berry problems

  • Birds: the biggest thief of blueberries, cherries and grapes. Netting draped over the plants as fruit colors is the only reliable fix.
  • Japanese beetles: skeletonize grape and raspberry leaves in July. Knock them into a bucket of soapy water in the early morning.
  • Spotted wing drosophila: a small fruit fly that lays eggs in ripening raspberries, blackberries and blueberries, leaving soft fruit with tiny larvae. Pick often, pick everything ripe, and refrigerate the harvest promptly.
  • Gray mold (Botrytis): fuzzy gray rot on strawberries and raspberries in wet weather. Good spacing, straw mulch and quick removal of damaged fruit help.
  • Poor fruit set on blueberries or elderberries: often a pollination issue. Plant two or more varieties.

Feeding berry plants

Most berries don't need heavy feeding. Too much nitrogen grows leaves at the expense of fruit and can make plants more prone to disease. For strawberries, brambles and grapes, a light application of balanced fertilizer or compost in early spring is usually enough. Blueberries prefer an acid-forming fertilizer labeled for azaleas, rhododendrons or blueberries, applied lightly in spring. A soil test every few years tells you more than any rule of thumb.

Ready to plant?

Small fruits arrive at the greenhouse in spring. Browse our berries and small fruit and fruit trees, and read our planting calendar for timing. Questions about a problem in your patch? Bring a sample in, or use the Purdue Plant & Pest Diagnostic Lab.

Frequently asked questions

What berries grow best in Indiana?

Strawberries, raspberries, blackberries, elderberries, gooseberries and grapes all grow well in central Indiana with basic care. Blueberries grow well too, but only after you make the soil acidic enough.

Why won’t my blueberries grow?

Usually soil pH. Blueberries need acidic soil, around pH 4.5 to 5.5, and many Indiana soils are much higher. Yellow leaves with green veins are the classic sign. Test the soil, add sulfur and acidic organic matter, or grow them in raised beds or large containers of acidic mix.

When should I plant strawberries in Indiana?

In early spring, as soon as the soil can be worked, usually April. Set plants so the crown is level with the soil surface, neither buried nor exposed.

How do I prune raspberries?

For summer-bearing raspberries, cut canes that fruited this year to the ground after harvest. For everbearing types like Heritage, you can mow all canes to the ground in late winter for one large fall crop, or prune only the fruited tips for a summer and fall crop.

Do I need two blueberry plants?

Planting two or more varieties that bloom at the same time improves pollination, berry size and yield. Planting early, mid and late varieties also stretches your harvest across the summer.
