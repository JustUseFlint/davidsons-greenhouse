Houseplant Care 101 (and the Best Pet-Safe Houseplants)

Light, water, humidity and repotting, explained plainly, plus which houseplants are listed as non-toxic for cats and dogs and which ones to keep out of reach.

Our greenhouse is green all year, and in the depths of an Indiana winter the houseplant room is the best place in town. Houseplants clean up the look of a room, give you something to tend in January, and are mostly easy once you understand what they need. This guide covers the basics, the most common problems, and the question we hear most often: is it safe for my cat?

Light: the most important thing

Light is food for plants. Most houseplant problems, from leggy growth to dropping leaves to no flowers, trace back to too little light, especially in Indiana winters when days are short and gray.

Reading your windows

WindowLightGood for
SouthBright, with direct sunSucculents, cacti, hoyas, crotons, ponytail palm, citrus and flowering plants
WestBright afternoon sun, can be hotSucculents, rubber plant, fiddle leaf fig (with a sheer curtain)
EastGentle morning sunMost houseplants: calathea, pothos, peperomia, ferns, prayer plant
NorthLower, indirect lightPothos, Chinese evergreen, peace lily, ZZ, snake plant

A plant 6 feet back from a window gets a fraction of the light it would on the sill. If a plant is stretching toward the window with long gaps between leaves, it wants more light.

Winter light

In winter, move plants closer to windows, clean dusty leaves (dust blocks light), and consider a grow light for sun-lovers. In summer, pull sun-sensitive plants back from hot south and west glass.

Watering: the most common mistake

More houseplants die from overwatering than underwatering. Roots need air as well as water, and soil that stays soggy suffocates them.

How to water

  1. Check first. Push a finger into the soil. For most tropical houseplants, water when the top inch is dry. For succulents and cacti, wait until it's dry all the way through.
  2. Water thoroughly. Water until it drains out the bottom, so the whole root ball gets wet.
  3. Empty the saucer. Don't let pots sit in water.
  4. Adjust for the season. Plants use much less water in winter. A plant that needed weekly water in July may need it every two to three weeks in January.

Drainage holes

Every pot needs one. Decorative pots without holes can hold a plastic nursery pot inside. Lift it out to water and drain.

Water quality

Some plants, including calathea, spider plant, dracaena and prayer plant, are sensitive to fluoride and minerals in tap water and show it as brown leaf tips. Rainwater, distilled water, or tap water left out overnight can help.

Humidity

Indoor air in an Indiana winter, with the furnace running, can be very dry, and many tropical houseplants come from humid forests. Signs of low humidity include crispy brown leaf edges, curling leaves and spider mites.

To raise humidity:

  • Group plants together. They create their own humid microclimate.
  • Use a pebble tray: a tray of gravel with water just below the top of the pebbles, with the pot sitting on the pebbles, not in the water.
  • Run a humidifier near humidity-lovers like calathea, ferns and nerve plants.
  • Use bathrooms and kitchens, which are naturally more humid, if they have enough light.

Misting gives only a brief boost and isn't a substitute for these.

Temperature

Most houseplants are happy at the same temperatures we are: about 65 to 75°F by day, a little cooler at night. Avoid:

  • Cold drafts from doors and leaky windows in winter
  • Hot, dry air from heat vents, radiators and fireplaces
  • Cold glass: leaves touching a window on a January night can be damaged

Feeding

Houseplants need nutrients while they're actively growing.

  • Spring through summer: feed every two to four weeks with a balanced houseplant fertilizer at half the label rate.
  • Fall and winter: reduce or stop. Plants grow slowly in low light, and extra fertilizer builds up as salts in the soil.
  • Flush the soil every few months by watering heavily to wash out salt buildup, which shows as a white crust on the soil or pot rim.

Repotting

Repot in spring or early summer when:

  • Roots circle the bottom or grow out the drainage holes
  • Water runs straight through without soaking in
  • The plant dries out much faster than it used to
  • It's top-heavy and tips over

Go up only one size, 1 to 2 inches wider. A pot that's too big holds excess wet soil and invites rot. Use fresh potting mix suited to the plant: a general mix for most tropicals, a chunky aroid mix with bark for monstera and philodendron, and a gritty cactus mix for succulents.

Pests

Check new plants carefully, and look under leaves when you water.

Spider mitesFine webbing, speckled or dull leavesRinse leaves; raise humidity; insecticidal soap
MealybugsWhite cottony clusters in leaf jointsDab with rubbing alcohol on a cotton swab; insecticidal soap
ScaleSmall brown bumps on stems and leaves; sticky residueScrape off; horticultural oil
Fungus gnatsTiny black flies around soilLet soil dry more between waterings; sticky traps

Isolate an infested plant from others until it's clean. At the greenhouse, every plant is checked for pests and disease before it reaches the sales floor, and we isolate anything that isn't healthy.

Pet-safe houseplants

If you share your home with cats or dogs, it's worth knowing which plants are listed as non-toxic. The best reference is the ASPCA's toxic and non-toxic plant list. Our garden guide flags pet-friendly plants, and every plant page on this site notes well-documented pet toxicity.

Listed as non-toxic to cats and dogs

  • Spider plant: easy and tough. Cats love to chew it, so it may look rough, but it's listed as non-toxic.
  • Hoya: waxy leaves and fragrant flower clusters.
  • Prayer plant and calathea: patterned leaves that fold up at night.
  • Peperomia: many shapes and textures, compact.
  • Chinese money plant (pilea): round, coin-shaped leaves.
  • Ponytail palm: a succulent "tree" with a swollen trunk.
  • Boston-type ferns: lush and humidity-loving.
  • Polka dot plant: splashy pink, red or white spotted leaves.
  • Air plants: grow without soil; generally considered non-toxic.
  • Venus flytrap: a carnivorous curiosity, listed as non-toxic.

Common houseplants that are toxic to pets

These are popular and beautiful, but keep them out of reach of pets that chew:

  • Pothos, philodendron and monstera: contain calcium oxalate crystals that irritate the mouth and cause drooling and vomiting.
  • Dumb cane (dieffenbachia): same irritating crystals, often more severe.
  • Peace lily: despite its name, not a true lily, but toxic because of oxalate crystals.
  • Aloe, jade and many succulents: toxic to cats and dogs.
  • Rubber plant, fiddle leaf fig and other ficus: irritating sap.
  • English ivy: toxic if eaten.

The serious one: lilies and cats

True lilies (Lilium, including Easter lilies, Asiatic and Oriental lilies) and daylilies (Hemerocallis) are extremely dangerous to cats. Even small amounts of leaf, flower or pollen can cause kidney failure. Keep them out of homes with cats entirely, including cut-flower bouquets.

If you think a pet has eaten a toxic plant, contact your veterinarian or the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center right away.

Easy houseplants for beginners

If you're just starting, these are forgiving:

  1. Pothos: low to bright light, tolerates missed waterings (toxic to pets)
  2. Chinese evergreen: handles low light
  3. Spider plant: easy and pet-safe
  4. Peperomia: compact, varied, pet-safe
  5. Hoya: thrives on a little neglect in bright light, pet-safe
  6. Succulents and cacti: perfect for a sunny window and infrequent watering

Moving houseplants outdoors for summer

Many houseplants love a summer outdoors, but move them gradually. Wait until nights stay above about 55°F, usually late May or early June, start in full shade, and give sun-lovers a week or two to adjust before full sun. Bring them back in before nights drop into the 40s in fall, and check for insects before they come inside.

Winter care for houseplants

Indoor air in an Indiana winter is dry and light is weak, so houseplants slow down. Water less often, because soil dries more slowly when growth stops. Hold off on fertilizer from late fall until new growth starts in spring. Move plants closer to windows (but not touching cold glass), and keep them away from heat vents and drafty doors. Grouping plants together raises humidity around them.

Frequently asked questions

What houseplants are safe for cats and dogs?

Spider plant, prayer plant, calathea, peperomia, Chinese money plant (pilea), hoya, ponytail palm, Boston fern, polka dot plant, African violets and many others are listed as non-toxic to cats and dogs by the ASPCA. Always check a specific plant on the ASPCA list, since common names can be confusing.

Which common houseplants are toxic to pets?

Pothos, philodendron, monstera, dieffenbachia (dumb cane), peace lily, ZZ plant, snake plant, aloe, jade, rubber plant and other ficus, and English ivy are all listed as toxic to cats and dogs. Most cause mouth irritation, drooling or vomiting. True lilies (Lilium) and daylilies are extremely dangerous to cats.

How often should I water my houseplants?

There’s no universal schedule. Water when the plant needs it, which for most tropical houseplants means when the top inch of soil is dry, and for succulents and cacti when the soil is completely dry. Plants need much less water in winter than in summer.

Why are the leaf tips on my houseplant turning brown?

Usually dry air, uneven watering, or salt buildup from fertilizer and tap water. Raise humidity, water thoroughly so excess drains out, flush the soil every few months, and use rainwater or filtered water for sensitive plants like calathea and spider plants.

When should I repot a houseplant?

In spring or early summer, when roots fill the pot, circle the bottom, or grow out the drainage holes, or when water runs straight through. Move up only one pot size (1 to 2 inches wider) and use fresh potting mix.
